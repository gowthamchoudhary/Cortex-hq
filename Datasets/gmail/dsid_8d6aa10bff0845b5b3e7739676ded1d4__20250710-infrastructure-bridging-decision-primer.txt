Rohit — thanks for the details and the topology. High level answers up front so your ops team can triage:

Short recommendation (starter): PrivateLink (interface endpoints) for control plane + Direct Peering for dataplane where low P99 latency is required. If your team cannot set up peering, use S2S VPN as a fallback for limited throughput.

Why:
- PrivateLink: strong isolation for control-plane telemetry and reduces need to open inbound rules; good for compliance and audit.
- VPC Peering: lowest latency if you can accept full-mesh routing and security review; simpler packet flow (no extra proxies) but scales poorly across many customer VPCs.
- S2S VPN: easiest with existing firewalls, but higher jitter/latency and operationally brittle for high QPS.

I'll loop in internal networking folks with tradeoffs and a quick action plan. Suggested next step: 1) confirm VPC CIDR overlap risk (if any), 2) indicate whether you can accept cross-VPC routing (peering) or prefer endpoint abstraction.

Tradeoffs summary (peering / privatelink / s2s vpn):

1) Latency and packet path
- VPC peering: best (no intermediate NAT or proxy). P99 expectations in-prod: 10–30 ms cross-AZ depending on cloud region. Ideal when your app and private nodes are both in same cloud region.
- PrivateLink: adds a proxy hop (AWS ENI + NLB path) — usually adds ~2–8 ms and is stable. Good when you want explicit service endpoints and don't want route table changes.
- S2S VPN: variable; typical additional jitter and 10–40+ ms added; not recommended for strict p99 goals.

2) Security & blast radius
- VPC peering: implicit routing; requires careful route management and can increase blast radius if not segmented per-VPC. No endpoint-level IAM.
- PrivateLink: service-level isolation, good for least-privilege. Traffic is private to provider endpoints. Works nicely with endpoint policies.
- S2S VPN: depends on firewall rules; encrypted but susceptible to NAT/MTU issues.

3) Operational complexity
- VPC peering: once set up, low ops. But CIDR overlap means no-go. Cross-account peering requires approvals.
- PrivateLink: more components (NLB, ENIs); our side needs to expose an endpoint service. Easier for customer to adopt without broad network changes.
- S2S VPN: quickest short-term but requires monitoring and failover automation (BGP recommended if throughput is moderate).

4) Scaling & multi-region
- Peering: not transitive; multi-region needs multiple peerings or backbone.
- PrivateLink: region-scoped endpoints; for multi-region you need regional endpoints.
- VPN: can be combined with transit/VPN concentrators but complexity rises.

Operational suggestion for EdgeHealth:
- Phase 1 (PoC): PrivateLink (control plane + low-volume dataplane) so your teams don't need route changes.
- Phase 2 (production): Add a peering link or direct cloud interconnect for high-throughput, low-p99 paths. Keep PrivateLink for control-plane telemetry and admin access.

Adding a security lens for your compliance team:

- Data in flight: all three patterns support TLS; PrivateLink and VPN provide strong channeling for minimizing internet egress.
- Identity & Access: PrivateLink allows us to put endpoint policies and require mTLS from the customer side. For peering, we recommend restricting SGs and using mutual TLS between services.
- Audit & logging: with PrivateLink we can produce ENI-level flow logs and tie AD audit events. For peering, you'll want VPC Flow Logs enabled on both sides and central log collection.
- Failover: If you adopt peering + PrivateLink, configure a health-checked failover route (e.g., route via PrivateLink NLB if peering path drops) — this is what we used for another customer to meet a 99.9% path SLA.

Operational asks for EdgeHealth:
1) Confirm whether you can accept a dedicated CIDR for Redwood endpoints (recommended).
2) Share any approved firewall egress ports (we typically need 443 + health-check ports).
3) Indicate if you have a transit/VPN concentrator or prefer per-VPC config.
