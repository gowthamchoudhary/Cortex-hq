Emma — following up with a more detailed methodology and initial numbers from our internal harness. I’m including the technical assumptions up front so you can map to Nimbus' environment.

Assumptions / test matrix
- Models: 7B-8B quantized (4-bit), 34B FP16, and 70B FP16. Model variants are similar to the open LLMs you listed in the RFP appendix.
- Hardware: A100-80GB and H100-80GB for 34B/70B tests; A100 or T4 for smaller models in dedicated pools.
- Sequence lengths: 32 tokens (short response) and 256 tokens (long response).
- Batching modes: continuous batching (default), fixed-batch-size (4, 8, 16), and single-request (no batching).
- Cache modes: cold start (no KV), warm start (prefix cache hit ~90%), and mixed trace (50% cache hits).
- Workload shape: Poisson-arrival synthetic trace + replayable production trace (we'll provide a 1-hour sample CSV).
- Measurement windows: 5-minute steady-state windows after a 10-minute warm-up for each point.

Key measured metrics (examples from our internal run on A100, 34B FP16):
- 32-token generation (single request, no batching): p50 ~ 45ms, p95 ~ 140ms, p99 ~ 260ms; tokens/s per GPU ~ 380.
- 32-token generation (continuous batching, batch target 8): p50 ~ 60ms, p95 ~ 170ms, p99 ~ 320ms; aggregate tokens/s per GPU ~ 1,800 (higher throughput, modest p99 tail increase if batch grows).
- 256-token generation (single request): p50 ~ 420ms, p95 ~ 1,200ms, p99 ~ 2,400ms; tokens/s per GPU ~ 140.
- Quantized 4-bit (same model family): latency delta typically +10–25% p50 vs FP16; throughput improvements ~1.8x–2.6x depending on kernel availability and sequence length.

Scaling behavior notes:
- Up to ~100 concurrent active requests per GPU (mixed lengths) we see near-linear throughput scaling with our autoscaler tuned for queue depth and observed latency SLOs.
- Past ~250 concurrent active requests (multi-GPU pool), efficiency depends on batching efficacy and KV cache hit-rate: with good cache hits and batching the platform sustains requests at 85–95% of theoretical throughput.
- Tail latency (p99) is most sensitive to heterogeneous request sizes and cold cache effects; we recommend a warm-up window and a 5–10% warm capacity buffer in autoscale.

Reproducible test steps (high level) — please see attached spreadsheet for concrete scripts:
1) Start with a baseline: single-client replay of 1k requests at target QPS for 10 minutes (no batching).
2) Run continuous batching tests with target batch sizes 4/8/16, measure p50/p95/p99 and effective tokens/s.
3) Toggle cache: cold vs warm; to simulate warm start, pre-warm by replaying the first 15 minutes of trace.
4) Concurrency sweep: 10 -> 1,000 concurrent virtual clients; collect per-GPU metrics (utilization, mem, kernel stall) and end-to-end latency distribution.
5) Canary run: mirror 2% of production traffic (sampled) for 24 hours against dedicated pool.

Artifacts we will provide for your SREs:
- k6/JMeter test scripts and sample CSV traces (redwood-load-test-plan.xlsx).
- Dockerized client harness to run in your VPC with a small sample dataset.
- Columnar CSV export format for results + plotting Jupyter notebooks.

Caveats and operational guidance:
- Quantized kernels improve throughput but can interact with attention kernel selection; verify quality on your evaluation prompts (we recommend embedding a small eval suite).
- Network tail (ingress) can dominate p99 in multi-region configurations — measure round-trip at your edge.
- For strict p99 SLOs under 256-token responses, prefer pinned Dedicated capacity and lower autoscale thresholds.
